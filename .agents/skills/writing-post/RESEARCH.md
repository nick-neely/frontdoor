# Research

Research supplies more than numbers. In the gold rush Post, the history callout, the Bainbridge quote, the DORA "amplifier" line, and the link back to an earlier Post were none of them in the draft, and each did as much work as the chart.

## Angles

Read the draft and fragments and choose the angles this Post can use. Each chosen angle gets its own subagent, and they all run in parallel.

| Angle | What it finds | Gold rush example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Evidence** | Data that backs a claim, or complicates it. | Faros review-time figures, the Stack Overflow trust split |
| **Lineage** | Older thinking on the same problem, with a quotable line. | Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (1983) |
| **Metaphor** | The real history behind the Post's leading metaphor or title. | Brannan and Levi Strauss selling to miners |
| **References** | The primary link for every tool, person, and work the draft names, plus this site's earlier Posts worth linking. | Matt Pocock's PR skill, the DDD Post |

Most Posts use two to four angles. An angle earns its subagent when the draft leans on it, so a Post with no figures to back skips Evidence, and a Post with a plain title skips Metaphor.

Give every subagent the half-remembered facts and suspected sources for its angle, so it verifies them rather than hunting blind. Ask each one for what complicates the argument as well as what supports it: the Levi Strauss jeans myth and the sign of METR's follow-up were both corrections the Post needed.

## The brief

Dispatch each subagent in the background with this brief, filled in for its angle:

```text
Use the `research` skill to gather sourced material for a Post titled "<title>".
Today is <date>. Your angle is <angle>: <what it finds, from the table>. Save
findings to `docs/writing/<stem>-research-<angle>.md` and edit no other file.

The Post's argument: <two or three sentences from the draft>.

This site publishes a number or a quote only when it appears verbatim in a
primary source. For every item, record the exact quoted sentence, the
population or sample and its date where there is one, the URL of the primary
source itself (the report, paper, or original page, not coverage of it), and
whether you read it on the primary page. Mark anything found only secondhand
UNVERIFIED. Prefer the newest edition of each report.

Find, in priority order:
<one numbered line per item, naming suspected sources and half-remembered facts>

Include anything you find that complicates the argument. End with a
"Recommended for the Post" section: the 3-5 strongest verified items, where each
would land in the draft, and the caveats a careful reader would raise
(different samples, vendor sources, correlation rather than cause, popular
myths).
```

## Using the results

Read every research file in full before adding anything. Start from each file's "Recommended for the Post" section. An item enters the Post verbatim, with its sample named where it has one and its primary source linked. A secondhand or UNVERIFIED item either stays out or goes on the hand-back fact-check list. A finding that complicates the argument goes into the prose, where it can sharpen the point instead of undermining it.
